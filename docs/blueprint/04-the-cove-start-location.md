# The cove

A 200 × 200 block cove in the Blue Ridge, about 10 acres at roughly 900 m. Laurel Spring rises at the head of the cove, and Hemlock Run carries every drop on the plot south to the outlet. Whatever happens on a slope ends up in the creek, which is why the creek is the heart of the game here.

## Fish

**Stream habitat units.** Every stream column is tagged by its depth and current:

- **Riffle:** shallow and fast, over cobble
- **Run:** medium depth and current
- **Pool:** deep and slow
- **Glide:** shallow and slow, over sand or silt

Each fish prefers certain units. A healthy trout stream alternates riffles and pools every 6–8 blocks.

**Water temperature**, per stream column, each game hour: T = T\_upstream + k × (T\_air\_24h\_mean − T\_upstream), with k = 0.02 × (1 − shade) per block of flow. Shade (0–1) comes from canopy and rhododendron over the column. Spring and seep inflows mix in at 12 °C by their share of the flow. Pools ≥ 4 levels deep run 1 °C cooler. Ponds ≥ 50 m²: T = T\_air\_24h\_mean + 2 × (1 − shade), with a 12 h lag. GameTest `fieldstation:fish/stream_temperature`: at a summer air mean of 22 °C, an unshaded 110-block reach ends near 21 °C (stressed) and a reach with 80% shade ends near 18 °C.

Trout are stressed above 20 °C and die above 25 °C.

**Two choices only the player can make.**

1. **The culvert.** Trout that drift below the perched Lower Culvert can't swim back up to the cold upper Run, so the lower Run is a dead end for them. Fixing the culvert reconnects the whole Run, but it also opens the way for rainbow trout from downstream. Real managers often keep a natural waterfall as a barrier. The player can rebuild the crossing so fish pass and add a log-and-rock cascade barrier just above the outlet, which brook trout can live with and rainbows can't climb.
2. **Miller Pond's fish.** Bass eat tadpoles, so a fished pond holds few frogs. Draining the pond with the pump (with a stocking permit to move the fish) makes it a fishless frog pond. Keeping the fish gives a warm-water fishery for contracts instead. Either is valid; the allotments adjust.

**Rules.** Fish only arrive through connected water and never spread over land. Stocking needs a permit. Fish numbers come from surveys only. Every reach starts with dace, sculpin and chub inside their start allotment. After the barrier is built, the plot's fish are a closed population.

## Stream insects and the stream health score

Most of the life in Hemlock Run is insect larvae living under the rocks. Volunteers in real Appalachian streams grade a stream by which of them they find, and the game uses the same idea.

| Group | Where | Tolerates silt and warmth | Role |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mayfly larvae | Riffles, on rocks | Poorly | Graze algae; late-spring and summer evening hatches feed trout, bats and swallows |
| Stonefly larvae | Riffles, under rocks | Very poorly | The best sign of a clean, cold stream |
| Caddisfly larvae | Riffles; build cases from sand or leaves | Poorly | Shred fallen leaves |
| Hellgrammites (dobsonfly larvae) | Under large stones | Moderately | Top insect predator; hellbenders and trout eat them |
| Crane fly larvae | Leaf packs in pools | Moderately | Shred leaves |
| Midge larvae and aquatic worms | Silty glides | Well | Show silt and low oxygen |
| Crayfish | Under rocks | Moderately | Eat everything; hellbenders' main food |
| Water pennies and riffle beetles | Riffle stones | Poorly | Need clean, oxygen-rich water |

**Kick-net sample.**

1. Stand in a riffle and hold the kick net downstream.
2. Shuffle the cobble upstream of it for 10 seconds.
3. Tip the catch into the sorting tray.
4. A sorting screen shows the larvae as simple pictures; drag each to its group.

Correct sorting gives the **stream health score** (0–10), based on how many sensitive groups turn up against tolerant ones. Mis-sorting lowers the score's reliability, not the stream.

**Stream health score.** Score = points for each group found: stonefly 3, mayfly 2, caddisfly 2, water penny or riffle beetle 1, hellgrammite 1, crane fly 0.5, crayfish 0.5; minus 2 if midges and worms are over half the catch; clamped to 0–10. A group lives in a reach when:

- stonefly: 7-day max water temperature < 18 °C, turbidity < 0.15, embeddedness < 20%
- mayfly: < 21 °C, turbidity < 0.3, algae film ≥ 0.2
- caddisfly: leaf input ≥ 0.2, turbidity < 0.3
- water penny: dawn O₂ > 7 mg/L, embeddedness < 30%
- hellgrammite: large stones, < 24 °C
- midges dominate when embeddedness > 50% or turbidity > 0.5

The upper Run starts near 8 and the lower Run near 3.

**What drives the insects.** Each stream reach holds insect numbers that respond to turbidity, temperature, oxygen, cobble cleanliness, leaf input from trees along the bank (shredders need leaves), and algae on rocks (grazers need it).

**What the score drives.** Trout, hellbender and waterthrush allotments, contract grades, and the Park Service's seasonal rating of the stream.

**Hatches.** On warm evenings in late spring and summer, mayflies rise from clean riffles as a particle swarm, and trout jump for them. A stream with no hatches is a stream in trouble.

**Mechanics.**

- **Rain nights.** On warm, rainy nights, salamanders come out onto the woods floor and can be counted along a transect with a red headlamp.
- **Cover boards.** Boards laid in the woods collect red-backed and dusky salamanders. Lifting them with the brush every few days gives a count.
- **Skin and disease.** Amphibians need wet gloves. Chytrid testing applies to all frogs and salamanders here.

**Timber rattlesnake rules.** It is rare, protected and important to the cove.

- It never strikes the player. If the player comes within 3 blocks, it rattles and holds still, and the HUD notes "Give it space".
- Staying within 3 blocks for more than 10 seconds stresses it, and it costs a little reputation.
- Its winter den at Raven Rock must not be disturbed: no digging or rock work within 8 blocks.
- A photo of one basking at the den in spring is one of the cove's rarest journal entries.

**Warmth mechanic.** Reptiles are only active once warm. On cool mornings they bask first. Players learn to look for them on sunny rocks in the late morning.

**Events.**

- **Dawn chorus.** From late spring, every territory sings at dawn. The chorus's loudness and variety work as an at-a-glance health check, and a 10-minute *point count* with the audio recorder turns it into data.
- **Woodcock sky dance.** On spring evenings, males spiral up over the field edge and tumble down twittering. It needs open field beside wet young woods.
- **Hawk migration.** On clear fall days, hawks stream along the ridge. Counting them from Raven Rock is a contract.
- **Chimney swift roost.** On fall evenings, swifts pour into the old chimney like smoke. Tearing the chimney down loses them.

**Nest sites.** Every nesting bird has a site rule: tree cavities, shrubs, the ground, structures or boxes. Mowing, clearing and building near an active nest in spring or summer costs reputation.

**Bats and white-nose syndrome.** Big brown bats, eastern red bats and tri-colored bats hunt over Miller Pond and the Run. Tri-colored bats have been devastated by white-nose syndrome, a fungal disease spread in caves where bats hibernate. There are no caves on the plot, so the player can't cure it. What the player can do is help survivors:

- summer roosts: the old chimney, bat boxes, snags
- plenty of insects: the mayfly hatches
- no disturbance of roosts

The journal tracks bat numbers through the bat detector, and a rising tri-colored count is a major win.

**Mast years.** Every 2–5 game years, oaks drop a huge acorn crop. The next year, deer, bear, turkey, squirrels and chipmunks rise, and so do their predators. In a failed mast year, bears roam more and raid bins. The forecast hints at mast in late summer.

**Tracks.** Mud on stream banks and fresh snow record tracks for a day. Photographing tracks logs species the player never sees.

## Fungi, moss, lichens and decay

Decay is how the cove feeds itself. Leaves and logs become soil, food and homes.

**Leaf litter.** Leaves fall in fall and form a litter layer. The litter breaks down over one game season. While it lasts, it holds soil moisture, shelters salamanders and millipedes, and feeds the soil (fertility rises). Leaves in the Run feed the shredding insects. Raking it all away, or jumping worms eating it, starves this whole chain.

**Log decay.** A fallen or felled log passes through 5 stages over about 3 game years:

| Stage | Looks like | Hosts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Fresh | Bark tight | Bark beetles |
| 2. Loose bark | Bark peeling | Salamanders under the bark, wood-boring beetles |
| 3. Soft | Wood breaks by hand | Red-backed salamanders lay eggs inside; fungi |
| 4. Crumbly | Moss-covered hump | Yellow birch and hemlock seedlings root on it (nurse log) |
| 5. Humus | A low mound of rich soil | Adds a topsoil layer |

**Mycorrhizal fungi.** Tree roots partner with soil fungi. Saplings planted in the old pasture grow at half speed until the soil has those fungi. Adding a shovel of woods topsoil to each planting hole brings the fungi along, a real restoration trick. Trees planted next to existing woods don't need it.

**Mushrooms.** After summer and fall rains, mushrooms fruit for a day or two: chanterelles, chicken-of-the-woods, turkey tail, coral fungi. Each is a journal entry. They feed box turtles, slugs and squirrels. None are eaten by the player, since there is no hunger.

**Moss and liverworts** grow on wet rocks in the Run's spray and on logs, and they hold moisture. They spread slowly and die if shade is lost.

**Lichens** crust Raven Rock and old tree bark. They grow very slowly and only in clean air, so they are a long-term air-quality sign in the journal.

**Slime molds** appear on wet logs after rain: odd, colorful, and a fun journal page.

## Algae, plankton and microbes

**Periphyton.** A thin film of diatoms and algae grows on the stream's cobble. Mayflies and snails graze it. Together with fallen leaves, it is the base of the stream's food web. Too much sun and nutrients turn it into long green strands that smother the riffles.

**Pond plankton.**

- **Phytoplankton:** floating algae, the source of blooms (see the Additions tab).
- **Zooplankton:** daphnia and copepods, which eat that algae.
- Small fish eat the zooplankton, and bass eat the small fish.

That makes a chain the player can see. With bass in Miller Pond, fewer bluegill eat zooplankton, so zooplankton clears the algae, and the water is clearer. Without bass, the opposite happens.

**Nitrogen.** Soil releases nitrate into water. Wetlands and marsh edges remove it: their bacteria turn it into gas. A marsh fringe on Miller Pond lowers the nitrate leaving the plot.

**What the microscope shows.** The microscope turns a sample into a short list of what's in it:

- diatoms, desmids and filamentous algae
- rotifers, daphnia and copepods
- chytrid zoospores (with stain)
- parasites

Each is drawn as a clean little card. Identifying them builds a microscope page in the journal and counts toward science credits.

**Chains the player can watch and change.**

| Chain | What breaks it | What fixes it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hemlocks → shade → cold water → trout | Adelgid kills the hemlocks; the water warms | Treat the hemlocks; plant rhododendron and yellow birch |

## Spring cleanup day (v0.3)

The Homeplace junk pile goes in the first 7 days (Clear the Homeplace). The cove still holds decades of trash: old tires, barbed wire, roofing tin and bottles along the old road, the drive, the Bench Field fence line and the Run. Real old dump sites keep "growing" as frost heave lifts buried glass and metal to the surface, and storms wash new litter down from the county road. Once a year, the player clears it.

**How it plays.** In spring, post a cleanup day on the RV computer, any day from spring day 3 to 7. The volunteer crew comes for 4 game hours, and everyone hauls trash to a drone crate at the pad or a field beacon. The player does the hands-on parts: cutting old wire off fence posts with the fencing pliers, rolling tires out of the Run, and crouching for 2 s to look under each sheet of tin or tire before lifting it.

| Rule | Number |
| --- | --- |
| Trash at the start | About 40 pieces at 5 sites: Skid Road, the old road edge on the west slope, the drive edge, the Bench Field fence line and the lower Run |
| New trash each year | Frost heave brings 3–6 old pieces to the surface at the old sites each winter. The spring storm washes 2–4 new pieces in at the upper edge and along the Run |
| Kinds | `fieldstation:trash` with component `kind`: tire, wire, tin, bottle, fence\_post or can |
| Carrying | Tires and tin go one at a time at 70% speed, like the wheelbarrow. Wire must be cut free first: 1 fencing pliers use per fence block |
| Crew | 2–4 helpers haul loose pieces. They never enter the Run or lift cover with an animal under it |
| In the Run | On cleanup day, trash in the Run or within 3 blocks of it needs no permit. On other days, the $40 stream work permit and the work windows apply |
| Look before you lift | Tin and tires are cover. 1 in 4 pieces at a woods or field site shelters an animal: a garter, rat or ring-necked snake, salamanders or a toad. Log it, then set the cover back or move it 1 block over |
| Cover boards | Up to 2 tin sheets per site may stay. They count as cover boards for salamander counts (Amphibians → Mechanics) |
| Mosquitoes | Tires hold water and breed mosquitoes. Each tire within 16 blocks of the RV adds 10% to the swarm chance there (Mobs → Nuisance mobs) |
| Disposal | The county takes everything on cleanup day at no charge. Metal comes back as scrap metal (4 scrap → 1 hardware kit) |
| History finds | 1 in 6 old pieces is a find: a mule shoe, a piece of chestnut rail fence, an embossed 1930s bottle, a crosscut saw blade. Each goes on the journal's History page with its site |

**Rewards.** Hauling 20 or more pieces on cleanup day: a $60 county litter grant and +10 reputation. Each history find: +2 reputation, plus 3 SC from v0.4. Clearing every tire within 16 blocks of the RV returns the swarm chance there to normal.

**Mistakes.** Lifting cover without looking makes the animal flee unlogged and costs −2 reputation. Hauling away occupied cover in spring or summer warns first, then costs −5 reputation, as the nest rules do. Skipping a year costs nothing: the trash waits, and the tires keep breeding mosquitoes.

**Hooks.** Clear the Homeplace (above), frost heave (Systems spec → Winter), the volunteer crew (Mobs → People), the mosquito swarm (Mobs), cover boards (Amphibians), scrap and hardware kits, drone crates and field beacons (Economy, crafting and drones), the stream work permit (Landscaping and terraforming), the History page (Player and tools → Field journal).

**GameTests.** `fieldstation:cleanup/frost_heave_surfaces`: after one winter, each old site surfaces 3–6 pieces, and none appear away from the sites. `fieldstation:cleanup/tires_and_mosquitoes`: 5 tires within 16 blocks of the RV raise the swarm chance there by 50%; hauling them returns it to normal.

## The old-timer (v0.5)

Arlo Haskett is a fictional retired neighbor in his eighties. He grew up at the Homeplace, and his family farmed the cove until the 1977 flood. Once a season he drives up, and if the player is around, he walks a few stops with them. At each stop he tells one short story, and each story points at something real on the plot.

He gives no quests, wants nothing, sells nothing and asks for nothing. His stories go on the journal's History page.

**Visits.** Day 3 of each season. His pickup waits at the RV pad from 10:00 to 12:00. If the player comes within 16 blocks of the RV in that time, he walks. If not, he leaves a note on the RV door with one story written out, and tells the rest next season. Nothing expires.

**Walking.** He walks at 70% of the player's speed, and only on the drive, built trail tread, mown meadow and ground no steeper than 1 in 4. He tells each story at its stop. If he can't reach it, he tells it from the nearest point he can reach within 24 blocks ("I can't get up there anymore. See that gray stump?"), or else from the RV porch. When built tread reaches within 4 blocks of a stop, he walks all the way and adds one more line. If the player wanders more than 24 blocks off, he waits.

**Stories.** Three per visit, by season, over his first four visits.

| Visit | He says | Points at |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spring | "There was standing water at the foot of that slope every March. Daddy ditched it to plant corn." | The vernal pool site (11) at the Seep Line |
| Spring | "Mother dug ramps on the north slope every April, but never more than a mess." | The ramps patch |
| Spring | "Granddaddy skidded dead chestnut down that road with a mule after the blight. It's been washing ever since." | Skid Road Gully |
| Summer | "Every June the box turtles crossed the drive to lay eggs in the sand by the garden." | The drive, and a sandy nesting bank site at the Homeplace |
| Summer | "Before the county put that pipe in, the branch ran open across the drive and we forded it. Trout came all the way up." | The Lower Culvert |
| Summer | "The house burned in '51, but the chimney stood. The swifts never missed a summer." | The old chimney |
| Fall | "Grandmother called this one a Limbertwig. It kept in the cellar till spring." | The old apple tree; names its variety (Plants → Heirloom orchard) |
| Fall | "We stayed off Raven Rock in the fall. The rattlers den up there." | The rattlesnake den |
| Fall | "In the November '77 flood the pond went over the dam and took the lower field. We quit farming after that." | Miller Pond's dam |
| Winter | "We kept the milk and butter in the springhouse. That water came out the same cold all year." | The springhouse ruin |
| Winter | "That gray stump was the biggest chestnut on the place. It still sends up sprouts." | The chestnut sprouts |
| Winter | "The surveyor cut that mark in the white oak. Everybody used it for the line." | The witness tree |

The real history behind them: chestnut blight swept the Southern Appalachians in the 1920s and 1930s, and dead chestnut was salvaged for years after. Chestnut roots still sprout. Box turtles nest in early summer. Timber rattlesnakes den in sunny rock crevices. Heavy rain caused major floods across the region in early November 1977. Spring water stays near the year's average air temperature. A "mess" of ramps is enough for one meal.

**After the first year.** Each later visit he walks one stop and says one "then and now" line about what changed most since his last visit: the trout count, the spring's flow, the gully, the swifts, the vernal pool, the orchard. He keeps coming as long as the game runs.

**Rewards.** Each story: a History entry with a map pin, +2 reputation and 3 SC. The pin marks the area within 16 blocks, not the exact spot. It is a marker, not a task, and nothing reminds the player about it. When the player later logs or finds what a story points at, its entry is stamped Seen: +3 SC. The extra line at a stop reached by trail: +2 reputation. Each "then and now" line: +1 reputation.

**Mistakes.** None to make. Missing him costs one season's walk, never a story.

**Hooks.** People (Mobs), hidden discoveries and restoration projects (this tab), built tread (Landscaping and terraforming → Trail craft), the History page (Player and tools → Field journal), the heirloom orchard (Plants), the Chronicle (Fun, feel and finish).

**Build.** Entity `fieldstation:old_timer`, built like the other People: villager AI with trading off, dialogue panels. His pickup is a parked decoration entity, `fieldstation:old_timer_truck`, that appears and leaves at the pad and never drives on the plot. Stories are data at `data/fieldstation/old_timer_story/<id>.json` with `season`, `text`, `stop_anchor` (an authored place), `pin_radius_blocks` and `points_at` (a discovery or project id).

**GameTests.** `fieldstation:old_timer/note_when_away`: with nobody near the RV from 10:00 to 12:00 on day 3, one story is logged from the note and the other two are told next season. `fieldstation:old_timer/trail_reach`: a stop 60 blocks up a 1 in 3 slope is told from the porch, and from the stop itself once built tread reaches within 4 blocks of it.

## Building the cove in Minecraft

**Authored layout, seeded detail.** The 11 places on the map are always where the map shows them, so the cove can be designed, tuned and tested as one place. Everything else varies by world seed: the exact land shape within each zone, rock and log placement, plant patches, and the starting size of each population inside its range.

**Generation steps.**

1. **Shape the land.** A cove function sets the height of each column: a narrow valley floor, steep side slopes, a cove head, Raven Rock and the Bench Field shelf, plus seeded noise.
2. **Lay the ground.** Each column gets its layers from the zone's recipe: topsoil, subsoil, clay, colluvium, bedrock.
3. **Carve the water.** Hemlock Run's bed is carved and lined with cobble and gravel, with a riffle and pool sequence. Laurel Spring and the seeps are placed.
4. **Place structures** from templates: the RV, the Homeplace chimney and junk pile, the springhouse ruin, Miller Pond's dam and silt, the perched culvert, the logging road, the stone walls.
5. **Plant.** Trees, shrubs and ground plants go in by zone recipe, including the dying hemlocks, the blackberry thickets and the stiltgrass edge.
6. **Seed life.** Every habitat record starts with populations inside its starting allotment, plus one hellbender under the Big Rock.
7. **Blend the edges.** The plot fades into vanilla mountain terrain outside the boundary, with survey pins marking the corners.

**Testing.** One GameTest (`fieldstation:world/cove_builds`) builds the cove from a fixed seed. It checks that every one of the 11 places exists, that the Run flows to the outlet, and that every habitat record has its starting population.

GameTest `fieldstation:habitat/vernal_pool_hydroperiod`: a pool built to the project spec holds ≥ 2 levels until summer day 4 in a normal year, and is dry by fall day 7 in at least 2 of 3 simulated years. The drying is what keeps bullfrogs out, as in real vernal pools.
